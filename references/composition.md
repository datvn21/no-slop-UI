# Composition follows the material

These are starting postures, not templates to complete. Choose according to the main subject, amount of real content, and user's task. Combine only when the content genuinely changes mode. The hero/features/cards/CTA sequence is a valid result only if those sections each earn their place.

| Material and task | Useful posture | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One product action can be tried immediately | **Workbench:** a working control or sample result takes the main area; explanation sits beside or below it. | Fake product screenshots or dead demo controls. |
| A physical place, product, food, or craft has strong real photography | **Image-led:** a few large images establish the subject; factual details and action remain close. | Mood photography that tells nothing about the actual offer. |
| A schedule, menu, catalog, inventory, or collection is the product | **Index-first:** real entries, filters, and dates carry the first view. | A decorative hero that delays the information people came to find. |
| A service is understood through steps or a concrete outcome | **Case-led:** show an actual case, artifact, or before/after with its source. | Generic numbered steps and invented testimonials. |
| A person or studio is judged by work | **Portfolio-first:** lead with selected work and meaningful captions; use an about section to add context. | A large abstract mission statement ahead of the work. |
| An app centers on a table, timeline, map, editor, or document | **Object-first:** give the working object most space; tools and metadata support it. | Dashboard cards that scatter one task across unrelated panels. |
| Users must compare options | **Comparison-first:** align meaningful differences and tradeoffs; keep labels and units clear. | Identical cards that hide the differences. |
| A long explanation is genuinely necessary | **Document-first:** readable type, section navigation, examples, and clear measures. | Adding cards and illustrations to break up text without improving comprehension. |

## Build a hierarchy that survives styling

1. Order the facts and actions by the user's path. Identify what must be visible before they can act.
2. Sketch the main viewport in grayscale or plain blocks: subject, action, supporting evidence, navigation. Use real content length where possible.
3. Group related information with proximity. Within-group spacing should be visibly smaller than between-group spacing. Add a border or surface only when it clarifies a boundary.
4. Choose one focal point. Other elements may be vivid, but they must not compete for the same attention at the same moment.
5. At small widths, reflow by priority. Do not merely reduce font sizes; preserve labels, key data, and the primary action.

For a multi-screen product, share tokens, component behavior, and navigation, while allowing each screen's content to determine its composition. Repeating the same card grid everywhere is consistency of a template, not consistency of a system.

## Deliberate variation

When consecutive pages all look alike, compare their first views across six axes: **subject, reading order, density, image-to-text balance, action placement, and surface structure**. Change an axis only where the content supports it. Variation is useful when it makes the material easier to recognize, not when it adds arbitrary novelty.
